Loop: Closing the Screening-to-Care Loop

Future Health Digital Innovation Challenge · Population Health · Challenge 05

Xia (Candice) Wu · Solo prototype · Version 3.0 · 12 November 2026

1. Purpose and intended user

Loop is a care-coordination prototype for patient navigators and clinical operations teams following a positive fecal immunochemical test (FIT). It turns a recorded screening result into a visible sequence of accountable handoffs, reviewed actions and documented closure. It does not interpret a screening result as a cancer diagnosis or recommend treatment.

The prototype has six views: a screening cascade, a worklist, time to colonoscopy, group comparisons with a separate public-health map, a model lab and a scenario lab. The cascade measures where follow-up stops, and the worklist gives each stalled case an owner and a next action. The model lab contains a fitted day-30 follow-up predictor and Kaplan-Meier estimation, evaluated only on synthetic data. It predicts missed follow-up, not cancer risk. Current operational queues remain deterministic; there is no external AI service or clinically validated prediction system.

2. Clinical and evidence context

USPSTF recommends colorectal cancer screening for asymptomatic, average-risk adults aged 45 to 75, with different recommendation grades for ages 45 to 49 and 50 to 75. Positive stool tests require follow-up colonoscopy to realize screening benefit. This demo assumes eligibility rather than implementing a clinical eligibility engine. [S1]

A 39-organization US study of 32,769 people reported 51.4% follow-up colonoscopy within 180 days after a positive stool-based test. It included FIT and multitarget stool DNA, and enrolled ages 50 to 75; it is a motivation for a scenario, not a universal FIT benchmark or a direct estimate for ages 45 to 49. [S2]

Corley et al. found that colonoscopy in the 10-to-12-month and later groups was associated with greater odds of colorectal cancer and advanced-stage disease relative to 8 to 30 days. The observational association does not establish causation or a safe waiting interval. [S3] A separate measure-development study supports evaluating defined follow-up windows. [S4]

The 180-day indicator here is project-defined. It is not a certified HEDIS measure. NCQA has announced COF-E for measurement year 2027; reproducing official reporting requires its full specifications, exclusions and data requirements, which this prototype has not implemented. [S5]

3. Cohort and observation design

There is one fictional screening episode per person. All 5,000 participants are assumed to be asymptomatic, at average risk, aged 45 to 75 at order and due for screening. The demonstration uses six Iowa counties and is not representative of Iowa, the US or Abu Dhabi. The snapshot is fixed at 2026-11-06.

Order dates range from 2025-06-01 through 2026-11-01. Subsequent events are generated in a coherent order and censored at the snapshot. An appointment calendar date may be in the future if the booking was already recorded; a future procedure is never counted as completed. Negative results branch to routine screening. Invalid tests have a separate review queue. Missing evidence means not recorded in the demo, not proof of nonattendance.

The simplified cohort does not model repeated FITs, death, disenrollment, transfers, competing risks, changing eligibility or complete external-provider data capture. Before real-world measurement, these require a prespecified protocol, eligibility rules and ascertainment process. A full 180-day observation window is assumed available for mature synthetic cases.

4. Metric specification

Let t0 be the positive FIT result date, tc the recorded colonoscopy date, and A the snapshot date. Dates are ISO calendar dates; interval arithmetic uses UTC midnight to avoid daylight-saving errors.

  • Primary denominator: positive FIT episodes with A − t0 ≥ 180 days.
  • Primary numerator: denominator members with a recorded colonoscopy and 0 ≤ tc − t0 ≤ 180 days.
  • Primary rate: numerator / denominator. The interface shows a dash when the denominator is empty.
  • Recent positives: A − t0 < 180 days. Exclude from both primary numerator and denominator even if already completed; retain in the operational queues when applicable.
  • Median waiting time: median tc − t0 among all observed completers. This is a completers-only descriptive statistic, not a population median or survival estimate.
  • No booking recorded: mature positive episodes without a booking date / mature positive episodes. This is not labeled “never scheduled.”
  • Group completion: the same primary numerator and denominator within each displayed group. Show counts and flag groups with fewer than 20 eligible episodes. No significance tests, causal claims or adjusted disparity estimates are made.

The seven-step cascade reports observed milestone counts at A. After positivity, its procedure-completion row includes both timely and late procedures, and its closure row includes any completed, reviewed loop. The 180-day indicator is displayed separately. Rows 1 to 2 use all orders as the scale; rows 3 to 7 use positive FIT episodes. Differences between counts may include ongoing work and incomplete documentation as well as permanent loss to follow-up.

4.1 Patient-level gap accounting

For every mature positive episode, reconstruct the latest recorded stage at its own t0 + 180 days. Count only events dated on or before that cutoff. Assign each unfinished episode to exactly one transition: positivity to referral, referral to provider acceptance, acceptance to booking, or booking to procedure. Distinguish appointments scheduled beyond the cutoff from appointments already due with no procedure evidence. A later procedure preserves the historical missed-window classification while removing the case from the current procedure backlog.

In the fixed baseline, 157 mature positives produced 71 timely completions and 86 missed-window episodes. The latter decompose into 27 before referral, 7 before receiving-provider acceptance, 19 before booking, and 33 after booking. The largest stage accounts for 38.4% of missed-window episodes: 16 had calendar appointments beyond day 180 and 17 had appointments due without a completion record. Of the 86 missed-window cases, 12 later completed and 74 remain without procedure evidence at the snapshot.

Conditional stage counts are 157 → 130 → 123 → 104 → 71, giving conversions of 82.8%, 94.6%, 84.6% and 68.3%. These are fixed-window process counts. Later entrants have less remaining time; the complements are not permanent attrition probabilities. Parallel notification, acknowledgement and ownership gaps overlap and must not be added to the exclusive transition totals. Negative FIT results and recent positives are not failures.

Each result is traceable in analysis/patient_flow_audit.csv. The analysis also exports stage denominators, subgroup bottlenecks and descriptive synthetic support needs. No confirmed refusal, cancellation, transfer, no-show or verified outside-provider event was collected. Therefore the analysis locates missing progression evidence, but cannot identify permanent dropout or establish a root cause. The cohort was not altered after the results were seen. The figure is generated from the saved analysis.

4.2 Landmark prediction and time-to-event estimation

A fixed-protocol L2 logistic model (C=1, lbfgs) predicts no procedure by day 180 among people still without a procedure at day 30. Four binary predictors are reconstructed at day 30: referral recorded, receiving-provider acceptance, booking, and an already-known calendar appointment beyond day 180. Future milestones are excluded. Untimed barriers and ownership, as well as demographic characteristics, are not predictors. Of 157 mature positives, one completed before the landmark, leaving 156 eligible episodes.

The later approximately 30% of positive dates form an evaluation set of 47 people. To prevent outcome-availability leakage, training is restricted to 37 early episodes whose entire 180-day labels were available before the earliest evaluation prediction on 12 March 2026; 72 intermediate episodes are excluded. The model is frozen at that point. Earlier full-cohort inspection is disclosed, so this is retrospective development evaluation, not blind independent validation. No hyperparameter or feature tuning was based on evaluation performance.

Evaluation AUROC is 0.681 for logistic regression versus 0.777 for a fixed workflow-stage rule and 0.500 for constant probability. Logistic Brier score is 0.219 versus 0.273 for the training-prevalence baseline. The stage rule is a ranking score and has no Brier comparison. Fixed-prediction bootstrap intervals are 0.519 to 0.835 for AUROC and 0.183 to 0.260 for Brier; these exclude training and design uncertainty. At K=10, either strategy covers 8 of 22 missed-window outcomes. This is historical selection coverage, not preventable failures. Boundary ties are also averaged to show sensitivity to tie breaking. Probability calibration is reported separately; one medium-probability bin predicts 52.5% versus 36.8% observed. The model is not demonstrated superior to the rule. With only 37 training people, the known-beyond-window feature has zero variation and cannot be learned.

Kaplan-Meier estimation uses all 246 positives, with recorded procedure as event and administrative censoring at snapshot or day 180. The day-180 cumulative completion estimate is 45.2% (pointwise 95% interval 38.5% to 52.6%). Independent censoring is assumed; no competing events are modeled. This does not replace the mature-cohort primary metric. Subject-level event/censoring records, source digests, coefficients, predictions and evaluation figures are saved in modeling/. The model lab uses frozen baseline outputs; it does not alter live worklist ranking or trigger outreach. Method references are listed in the appendix.

5. Workflow and closure logic

The worklist sorts by elapsed days, with patient ID as a stable tie-breaker. Race, language, insurance and county are not priority inputs. Language and barriers inform a human's support planning, not access restrictions.

Demo operational thresholds are: over 30 days since order without kit return; over 14 days since positivity without referral; over 90 days since positivity without colonoscopy for navigation; over 180 days for escalation review; and over 14 days after colonoscopy without closure for a closure-review flag. These are configurable design assumptions, not universal clinical deadlines or urgency triage rules.

A case detail shows notification, acknowledgement, referral placement and receiving-provider acceptance, consultation, booking and calendar appointment, procedure, pathology, plan and review. Unassigned cases remain visible. A past appointment without a procedure triggers reconciliation rather than an automatic no-show label.

Closure requires all of: a recorded colonoscopy, pathology if a specimen was collected (otherwise not applicable), a documented follow-up plan, patient acknowledgement, an assigned owner, and human closure review. A reminder or acknowledgement never creates a clinical outcome. Closure review is unavailable until evidence prerequisites are present.

The user can assign an owner, record a barrier, simulate acknowledgement or referral acceptance, create an outreach draft, and approve simulated outreach only after checking a review confirmation. No message is delivered. The English template deliberately avoids diagnosis or detailed results; language-appropriate and culturally appropriate review is a prerequisite for any future deployment. The prototype does not demonstrate a validated multilingual communication system or live consultation.

6. Data generation and provenance

The generator uses Python's standard library and a fixed random seed, 20261106. It creates entirely fictional records without sampling, training on or transforming real patient data. It is not a Synthea execution or a validated disease model.

Illustrative inputs include a 77% eventual kit-return probability, 6.8% positive and 2.5% invalid results among generated result events, 84% referral placement, 93% acceptance conditional on referral, and 89% booking conditional on acceptance. Calendar delays, barriers and attendance are synthetic design choices. The baseline is literature-informed at the aggregate level, not fitted to individual observations. Demographic proportions and any resulting differences are arbitrary demonstration features. CDC prevalence is never used to assign an individual's outcome.

Reproducible baseline: 5,000 people; 3,597 valid FITs; 246 positives; 157 mature positives; 71 timely completions (45.2%); 89 recent positives; 100 observed colonoscopies; 12 later than 180 days; median 98.5 days among completers; 97 navigation-eligible cases; and 52 documented closed loops.

The independent map uses all 99 Iowa county crude prevalence estimates from CDC PLACES 2025 release, measure COLON_SCREEN. The underlying screening source year is 2022. These are model-based estimates of screening coverage among adults aged 45 to 75, not follow-up after positive FIT. The map remains fixed when fictional patient filters change. Raw data, retrieval date, dataset identifier and SHA-256 digest are retained. County geometry is distributed by Plotly using Census-derived boundaries and is for illustrative display. [S6 to S9]

7. Architecture, dependencies and reproducibility

The application is a static HTML/CSS/JavaScript bundle. The generator writes CSV, JSON, metadata and a local JavaScript data bundle. Shared metric and patient-pathway modules feed the views, standalone analysis and automated checks. Run npm run analyze after data generation to reproduce analysis outputs. Optional report rendering requires matplotlib 3.10+; the analysis itself uses Node.js only. The browser renders charts and county geometry without third-party runtime libraries.

Data flow: synthetic generator + public CDC snapshot → local data bundle → shared metric and model outputs → six dashboard views → human-reviewed local events. Clinical source milestones remain fixed; a local overlay records ownership, barriers, acknowledgement, acceptance and review actions. Each event has an identifier, timestamp, simulated actor, effective snapshot and action details.

Modern browsers can open index.html directly; a local HTTP server provides more predictable storage behavior. Runtime dependencies: browser JavaScript, native dialogs, SVG, Blob downloads and optional localStorage. Development dependencies: Python 3.9+ for data generation and Node.js 20+ for built-in tests. No npm package installation, API key, paid service, authentication or internet connection is needed for the bundled demonstration. Refreshing CDC source data does require network access.

The localStorage overlay is browser-specific and can be exported or reset. If storage is unavailable, actions continue in memory with a notice. It is neither shared across staff nor a tamper-resistant audit log. The prototype is hosted as static files at https://candicewu0515.github.io/loop-screening-to-care/ and needs no login.

7a. Simulation engine: events, visibility and constrained allocation

The simulation preserves the V1 cohort and its fixed-snapshot results. It reuses 246 positive-FIT index dates and demographic templates but generates new post-positive events and follows every episode for 180 days. Consequently, simulated rates must not be subtracted from V1 rates as an intervention comparison. Across seeds, these are repeated hypothetical worlds, not new independent real patients.

A custom discrete-event engine uses an event queue, patient-specific keyed random draws and a shared appointment calendar. It models routine contact attempts, documented support assessments, referral and handoff friction, booking, cancellations, nonattendance, rebooking, outside care, delayed or missing records, and reconciliation. Each event separates occurred_day from available_day. Prediction and task choice use only the observation projection; latent support needs, future procedures and final outcomes are withheld. A separate immediate scheduling-interface assumption exposes occupied capacity and the next available slot. Outside appointment reports never count as completed procedures.

Four policies run under identical resource ceilings: usual care (no added navigator), longest wait first, predicted noncompletion risk first, and action-aware allocation. The last policy uses a whole-task knapsack maximizing a declared heuristic utility under the weekly minute limit. It is not a learned treatment-effect model. Task costs range from 10 to 25 minutes. Navigation is eligible on days 30 to 150, with a 14-day cooldown and at most three tasks per episode. Outside-record verification waits until seven days after the known expected procedure date. These are experimental settings, not clinical recommendations. Usual care still includes routine clinical work; reported navigator minutes cover only added allocated tasks.

The calendar enforces weekly slots shared by the entire synthetic cohort. Cancellations release future slots; nonattendance consumes the reserved slot. Navigation cannot manufacture procedure capacity. Default dedicated capacity is four procedures and 120 extra navigator minutes per week. Base action advancement succeeds with probability 0.85, scaled by each scenario's effect multiplier (section 7b), before task-specific eligibility and reachability: an uncalibrated assumption. Trials support testing navigation, but their annual completion effects do not calibrate this experiment's 180-day changes. [S10, S11]

Risk modeling uses 16 time-available workflow features and day-30/60/90/120/150 landmarks. Repeated landmarks stay in the same world split, and splits are by whole world. Active-policy distribution shift remains a limitation because training uses usual-care trajectories. Training, selection and evaluation of the current models are described in section 7b.

Randomness is keyed by seed, patient, event type and attempt to support whole-world paired comparisons despite different event counts. Paired world-level t intervals summarize Monte Carlo variability conditional on assumptions; they exclude parameter and clinical uncertainty. Shared capacity induces interference between patients, so individual paths are not individual causal-effect estimates.

The scenario page presents mutually exclusive endpoint stages, true versus recorded completion, a time-controlled evidence replay and a budget-accounted sample week. It reads precomputed experiments; changing a selector does not rerun or deploy a policy. Predicted risk and heuristic utility are shown only where used. Synthetic demographics are reserved for subgroup auditing, not allocation. Latent mechanisms are independent of demographic groups, so subgroup differences cannot identify real inequities or establish fairness. There are no clinical, mortality, economic, or prospective-effect claims.

Engine tests check resource limits, zero-effect outcomes, event availability, hidden-state exclusion, cooldowns and accounting. The code uses Python's heap queue, not SimPy. Reproduction commands for the current experiment are given in section 7b.

7b. Current experiment (V3): corrected workflow, label ascertainment and policy ablation

The Scenario lab shows this experiment. An earlier iteration is archived outside the delivered prototype; only its frozen model is retained here, as a comparator. Two regression cases in that iteration revealed operational defects: an overdue internal appointment without procedure evidence could yield no verification task; an earlier nonattendance could override a later cancellation in the rebooking category. V3 verifies internal overdue appointments after a seven-day grace period and classifies by the latest occurred, available failure event. Missing evidence still does not prove nonattendance. A separate 12-seed paired old-mechanism comparison isolates these repairs from other mechanism changes.

V3 tests whether earlier high scores depended on artificial certainty. Blocked referral/acceptance/booking stages can recover without extra navigation (default weekly probability 0.04), and an outside appointment can fail to complete (default completion probability 0.80), returning to the internal workflow. Default navigation advancement is 0.85 times a 0.50 effect multiplier before reachability and eligibility. These are fixed, uncalibrated sensitivity assumptions, not newly estimated clinical parameters.

The policy model now predicts absence of a timely procedure record by day 180, trained and selected using the record-based label that a clinical dataset might actually provide. Ideal simulator truth is reserved for evaluation and a separate diagnostic comparator. Twelve training worlds, four validation worlds and 24 test worlds use disjoint seeds. Fixed logistic and random-forest candidates are selected by validation day-60 record-label Brier score and frozen. The selected random forest is not claimed to predict a causal benefit from intervention. All models are scored separately against actual and recorded noncompletion, with world-level AUROC, AP, Brier, mean prediction and observed rates plus descriptive calibration bins.

The frozen model from the earlier iteration reaches actual-outcome AUROC 0.803 in the revised standard mechanism, compared with 0.972 in its original mechanism; under faster routine recovery it reaches 0.738. A supplementary comparison using the same 24 fresh seeds and the same frozen model yields AUROC 0.973 under old mechanisms versus 0.803 under revised mechanisms. Day-60 eligibility can also change, so this is a scenario-level comparison. This is simulated mechanism transport, not external clinical validation. The new record-label model has actual-outcome AUROC 0.794; the ideal truth-label diagnostic reaches 0.858. The record-label model's mean prediction is 58.7% while actual noncompletion among its day-60 audit set is 49.2%; the discrepancy partly reflects a different target, not necessarily poor calibration for missing records. Its predictions must not be relabeled as actual care-gap probabilities.

Eight conditions, four policies and 24 paired seeds yield 768 main worlds. Five task-package ablations under standard and tight capacity add 240 worlds; 24 repair-only runs bring the evaluation total to 1,032. Direct paired comparisons with longest-wait allocation replace informal claims of model-priority superiority. Standard true completion is 57.4% under usual care, 76.5% under longest wait, 76.3% under record-gap risk priority and 76.2% under task allocation. Record-gap risk priority minus longest wait is -0.17 percentage points (conditional world interval -0.39 to +0.05); task allocation is -0.25 (-0.45 to -0.06). These are conditional simulation comparisons, not clinical significance findings.

Removing coordination from the full longest-wait policy decreases standard completion by 13.5 percentage points; removing verification mainly reduces recorded completion. With two slots per week, longest-wait navigation minus usual care is -1.30 points (interval -3.75 to +1.15), so stable improvement is not established. Removing preparation/transport support in that constrained setting reduces completion by 7.45 points relative to full navigation. Ablations change workload reallocation and shared-capacity competition; their effects are not isolated task effects and must not be added.

After observing congestion, 192 diagnostic replays of the same seeds traced calendar use without tuning policies. Under two slots/week, mean booking delay increases from 70.0 days in usual care to 113.8 with full longest-wait navigation; the mean number scheduled beyond the individual 180-day window rises from 31.5 to 83.8. This supports congestion as a mechanism within this simulator, not a recommendation to withhold real care. Resource occupancy spans the full world horizon and can include procedures beyond a person's day 180; it is kept separate from the primary outcome denominator.

Remaining uncertainties include ascertainment of real outcomes, local event and intervention parameter calibration, active-policy distribution shift, staff and patient behavior, competing events, transportability and clinical effectiveness. No clinical net-benefit curve is asserted: shared capacity, heterogeneous tasks and unknown benefit/harm weights require an explicit policy model beyond a binary risk threshold. The results figure and the complete world-level outputs are linked from the Scenario lab. Reproduce using scripts/simulation_v3/experiment_v3.py, capacity_diagnostics.py, transport_diagnostic.py and report_v3.py. Navigation tasks stop at day 150, so late record reconciliation needs a continued operational workflow beyond this early-navigation experiment. The V3 development revision also corrects ascertainment after an outside-to-internal transfer; all outputs were rerun with the same parameters and seeds.

8. Data governance, safety and limitations

Only synthetic patient-level records and publicly available aggregated county estimates are included. No names, contact addresses, dates of birth, real patient identifiers or protected health information are stored. There is no analytics tracking, remote model invocation, messaging service or EHR connection. Explicit synthetic labels appear in the interface and exports use synthetic IDs.

The system is an educational workflow prototype, not for clinical use. It makes no diagnosis, treatment, clinical-effectiveness or regulatory-classification claim. Elapsed-time flags do not replace clinical triage; urgent and exceptional cases require a qualified professional's pathway, which is not implemented here.

Limitations include artificial attendance and barrier assumptions; small subgroups; fixed single-episode follow-up; incomplete modeling of cross-provider evidence; no clinical integration; no verified patient identity, consent management, access control, authentication, role permissions or durable audit infrastructure; and no prospective user or clinical evaluation. Visualized demographic differences are not real-world disparity findings. The English-only interface is an initial demo, not a culturally validated communication tool.

For a pilot, require approved data access and use, local clinical governance, minimum necessary data, consent/communication preferences, authenticated role-based access, encryption, immutable event history, retention rules, outside-provider verification, incident response and documented responsibility across organizations. US screening evidence and geography must be replaced or contextualized for the local pathway and jurisdiction; these data do not establish Abu Dhabi need or effect.

9. Evaluation, scaling and pilot path

Current validation includes 50 automated checks covering exact metric boundaries, recent-positive exclusions, empty denominators, negative branching, closure prerequisites, chronology and uniqueness across all 5,000 records, queue sorting, the 99-county data join, exclusive gap reconciliation, historical cutoffs, late recovery, subgroup accounting, landmark feature availability, outcome-availability separation, censoring, coefficient reproduction and capacity coverage. Browser review covers navigation, cohort filtering, search, draft approval, local audit behavior, responsive layout and patient drill-down from gap stages. Simulation tests add invariant checks of resource limits, reproducibility, hidden-state isolation, zero-effect behavior, complete experimental design and patient-to-world accounting. Functional correctness on simulated data does not establish improved patient outcomes.

Proposed users are patient navigators and screening-program managers. A potential buyer is a health system or screening-program operator; an annual service with integration/support is a commercial hypothesis, not validated willingness to pay. A small pilot should first measure workflow usefulness and burden with approved data, before making clinical or economic impact claims.

Suggested pilot sequence: (1) interview navigators and clinicians and agree ownership and escalation rules; (2) run a read-only shadow workflow and audit missing/outside-provider records; (3) test human-approved task handling with local authorization; (4) evaluate a prespecified, adequately observed cohort. Compare time to notification/acknowledgement, referral acceptance, appointment completion, unresolved results and evidence closure; measure duplicate outreach, inaccurate flags, staff time and patient understanding as balancing outcomes. Select a justified comparator and adjust for calendar and case-mix changes rather than attributing any pre/post difference to the software alone.

Scaling requires an episode store, ingestion and deduplication, terminology mapping, authenticated provider access, an event service, and secure task/messaging integration. Candidate clinical resources include orders, observations, procedures, reports, tasks and communication records; these mappings remain a design proposal, not an implemented interoperability claim. The next evidence milestone is successful navigator use and reliable cross-provider reconciliation, not a claimed reduction in cancer or mortality.

Simulation assumptions

The 6.8% positive-result input, return/referral/booking probabilities, demographic mix, diagnostic findings, barriers, calendar delays and workflow thresholds are simulation choices made for this project. They are not estimates of prevalence or diagnostic accuracy, and results from the simulation are not evidence of any improvement caused by Loop.

These references describe methods. They are not evidence that the model works on real patients. All evaluation results are reported, including the rule baseline that outperformed the model.

Audit method

  • Evaluation of clinical prediction models (part 2): how to undertake an external validation study, BMJ 2024. Supports separately assessing discrimination, calibration and decision usefulness. The current experiment audits a simulated change of mechanism and a change of label. It is not an external clinical validation and does not claim TRIPOD compliance.

Natural recovery, outside appointment failure and revised navigation response are prespecified simulation assumptions. No new clinical data were used to estimate them. The audit looks separately at what the model is for, which labels a clinic could obtain, and how policies behave when patients share resources.
